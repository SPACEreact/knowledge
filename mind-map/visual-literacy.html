Before studying art, study the eye and brain receiving it. Composition, colour, camera and cuts work because they pass through a perceptual machine.

The goal is internalization: feel that an image is wrong, locate the variable, know what to change.

I

The machinery of seeing

Attention is selected, not received.

The perceptual machine

The eye samples. The brain predicts, groups and fills gaps. Seeing is an active construction shaped by memory and expectation.

Four salience drivers

Contrast differs from its field. Isolation breaks a group. Motion announces change. Expectation makes surprise visible.

Gestalt grouping

Proximity · similarity · closure · continuation · figure/ground · common fate. The mind prefers the simplest stable organization.

Depth without depth

Occlusion · relative size · atmospheric perspective · convergence · height in frame · focus. Flat marks become space.

Processing fluency

Clarity is processed faster and often feels more truthful. Difficulty can create attention—but only when the friction is meaningful.

Two-second recall

Look briefly, hide the image, then name: first eye landing, structural shape, dominant temperature and remaining emotion.

II

Colour

A wavelength enters. A relationship is felt.

Physical / perceptual

Hue comes from wavelength; colour experience comes from adaptation, illumination and neighboring colour.

Association / culture

Colour carries learned meaning, but no universal dictionary. Red may signal love, danger, fertility, power or mourning depending on context.

Pigment history

Ochre, indigo, madder, lapis, vermilion and synthetic dyes changed what cultures could picture—and what power could afford.

Breaking “bad colour”

Clashing colour works when hierarchy, proportion and purpose are controlled. Discord can be the story.

Read the whole field

Ask what dominates, what recedes, what repeats, and which accent alters the emotional temperature.

Colour is relational

Never judge a swatch alone. Change background, scale, material and light before deciding what it means.

III

Composition

The frame distributes pressure.

Visual weight

Size, contrast, colour, faces, detail and isolation add weight. Balance is an equilibrium of unlike forces—not symmetry.

Negative space

Empty space is active. It can create calm, dignity, loneliness, suspense or room for an unseen threat.

Leading lines

Edges, gazes, roads and light paths aim attention. The strongest line is the one the viewer follows without noticing.

Triangle through history

Stable pyramids organize sacred and royal imagery; tilted triangles convert stability into urgency.

Deliberate imbalance

Put weight near an edge, leave an answer absent, or oppose movement with space. Instability becomes emotional syntax.

Structural reading

Reduce a frame to masses and directional lines. The skeleton remains when subject matter disappears.

IV

Light & value

Light describes form—and judges it.

Position

Front, side, back, top and under-lighting expose different facial structures and different moral readings.

Ratio

A low key-to-fill ratio feels open and legible. A high ratio increases mystery, separation and threat.

Silhouette

When internal detail disappears, pose and contour must carry identity, intention and movement.

Chiaroscuro

From sacred painting to noir, extreme value contrast has staged revelation, guilt, power and concealment.

Separation

Value, colour temperature, edge light and atmosphere keep subject and background readable.

Moral commentary

Lighting never merely exposes. It tells us who may be trusted, what remains hidden and where grace enters.

V

Shape, line & space

Geometry arrives before character.

Primitive forms

Reduce bodies, sets and graphics to circle, square, triangle and organic form. Repetition creates a world; deviation creates a character.

Line direction

Horizontal rests. Vertical asserts. Diagonal accelerates. Curves guide and embrace. Jagged lines agitate.

Space & lens

Wide lenses expand near/far difference; long lenses compress distance and stack planes. Space itself becomes a relationship.

VI

The camera

Perspective is a psychological position.

Camera height

Low can enlarge power; high can expose fragility; eye level can share space. Context can reverse each reading.

Focal length

Wide lenses exaggerate spatial difference and facial depth. Long lenses flatten space and turn distance into observation.

Depth of field

Shallow focus isolates and withholds; deep focus allows relationships to compete in the same moment.

Movement

Pan reveals, dolly changes relation, handheld transmits instability, locked frames refuse intervention.

Motivation

Move because attention, power or emotion changes—not because the equipment can.

Point of view

Every placement answers: whose knowledge, whose distance and whose emotional risk organizes this image?

VII

Movement, time & editing

The cut is an act of thought.

Matchform continuesActionmotion continuesEyelinegaze resolvesGraphicshape rhymesContrastmeaning collidesEllipsistime disappears

Anticipation

A preparatory action makes the main action legible. The viewer reads intention before consequence.

Follow-through

Parts continue after the primary force stops. Residual motion gives weight, material and life.

Six cuts

Match, action, eyeline, graphic, contrast and ellipsis each preserve or rupture a different continuity.

Shot duration

Shortening creates acceleration; extension creates observation, discomfort or reverence. Rhythm is relational, not a fixed number.

The long take

Refusing the cut preserves geography, duration and moral responsibility. The viewer cannot escape by editorial permission.

Movement grammar

Timing, spacing, easing and direction shape whether motion feels heavy, graceful, comic, mechanical or afraid.

VIII

Blocking & mise-en-scène

Position turns relationship into architecture.

Blocking tells story

Who approaches, withdraws, crosses, turns away or occupies the center reveals power before dialogue does.

Group patterns

Lines create parity, triangles hierarchy, clusters belonging, splits conflict and depth staging unequal knowledge.

Cinema history

Tableaux borrowed theatrical clarity; montage fractured space; deep staging let multiple actions coexist.

Mise-en-scène

Set, costume, light, body, lens and frame operate together. Changing one variable alters the reading of all others.

Power through position

Centre is not always power. The figure who controls entrances, gaze or distance may control the scene from an edge.

Synthesis

Read a frame as a system: attention → relation → emotion → story function.

IX

Production design, costume & texture

A world remembers who lived in it.

StonepermanenceWoodwarmthMetalprecisionGlassdistanceFabricbodyEarthorigin

Room as biography

Wear, repair, clutter, absence, status objects and inherited objects reveal time, class and private rituals.

Costume arc

Silhouette, fit, repetition, deterioration and colour can make transformation visible before a character admits it.

Material meaning

Stone, wood, metal, glass, fabric and earth carry physical behavior first; association grows from use and context.

Human scale

Architecture feels intimate, civic, sacred or oppressive through its relation to the body.

Texture & light

Roughness breaks highlights; polish reflects worlds; translucency holds light. Texture becomes visible through illumination.

Designed history

A convincing set contains causes: what was built, used, neglected, protected and changed.

X

Contrast & visual rhythm

Difference catches the eye. Repetition teaches it where to look.

Light / darkLarge / smallWarm / coolSharp / softDense / sparseStill / movingRegular / irregularNear / far

Contrast matrix

Value, size, colour, edge, density, motion, pattern and depth can all create emphasis. Stack them cautiously.

Repetition

A repeated interval becomes expectation. Variation becomes event. The break gains force from the pattern before it.

Hierarchy

Primary, secondary and tertiary information must remain distinct. If everything shouts, nothing leads.

XI

Symbolism & visual meaning

A motif earns meaning through transformation.

Frame states

Open, divided, enclosed and released frames can chart a character’s changing relation to the world.

No symbol dictionary

Meaning is contextual. A door may mean escape, exclusion, ritual, danger or nothing until the story makes it recur.

Motif transformation

Introduce, repeat, vary and resolve. A motif becomes dramatic when its context changes while its form remains recognizable.

The red web

Trace one colour through costume, space, object and light. Its meaning moves from appearance to relationship.

Symbol as consequence

Begin with character and action. Let the symbol condense meaning already alive in the work.

Restraint

A motif does not need explanation. Repetition, placement and transformation teach the audience how to read it.

XII

Artistic intuition

Vocabulary disappears into choice.

1Feel2Notice3Name4Control5Combine6Break7Invent

Vocabulary levels

“This feels wrong” becomes “the edge tension and warm/cool split oppose the scene’s intimacy.” Precision enables revision.

Spaced training

Return to one variable across many images and days. Recognition grows faster when memory must retrieve, not merely reread.

Mastery

Intuition is compressed experience: principles retrieved quickly enough to feel immediate.

Ten five-minute studies

  1. Look for two seconds; recall the first eye landing.
  2. Reduce a frame to three value masses.
  3. Name its structural skeleton.
  4. Find the dominant warm/cool relationship.
  5. Trace every gaze and leading line.
  6. Rebuild the relationship using different objects.
  7. Predict where the eye lands, then check.
  8. Remove one element; predict the hierarchy shift.
  9. Recreate a studied image from memory.
  10. Make a romantic frame threatening without changing the actors.

“This feels right” is not the end of analysis. It is analysis becoming instinct.
